Behind the verdict: NVIDIA starts paying for the electricity, not just the chips

2026-08-23

NVIDIA starts paying for the electricity, not just the chips

Memory still caps how many chips get built, and nothing moved that today. What moved is where the seller is putting its money: power plants and ready-to-build land.

1

The ceiling has not moved

Little changed today about why supplyⓘ is not catching up. NVIDIA's memory suppliers can still meet only 60% to 70% of the stacked-memory volume asked of them for the Rubin Ultra generation, as reported on August 7. Micron said at its August 10 forum that it can supply no more than about half of customer demand, calls this memory the number one limit its biggest customers face, and has no line of sight to when supply meets demand. TrendForce said on August 4 that memory supply stays short through 2027, which caps how much factory capacity can be turned over to the stacked kind at all.

Rental prices are the fastest read on whether any of this is loosening, and today they are flat. Lambda's price list, captured today, shows an H100 at $3.99 to $4.29 per chip per hour and a B200 at $6.69 to $6.99, with reserved clusters starting at $5.54 and $8.87. The same seller showed the same $3.99 and $6.69 low end on August 19, and its newest publicly rentable part is still the B200: no H200, no GB200, nothing from AMD. Nobody is discounting chips they cannot build enough of.

The chip seller buys into power and land

The news today is about electricity.ⓘ NVIDIA will invest up to $3 billion in Lancium, the power developer behind the Stargate campus in Abilene, Texas, taking about 20% ownership for an initial $2 billion. Separately, it is in advanced talks to put several hundred million dollars into Cloverleaf Infrastructure, which develops data-centre land that already has power connected, has sold more than 7 gigawatts and has a pipeline above 10 gigawatts. Both were reported on August 21.

This follows the same pattern set a few days earlier. On August 17 NVIDIA guaranteed SB Energy's Ohio campus to host only NVIDIA machines, an initial 4.25 gigawatts with an option to reach 8, with its payment obligation capped at $105 billion and paid out only if OpenAI defaults. On the same day it emerged that NVIDIA had cut that promise from a proposed $250 billion to under $120 billion, covering only the first phase. On August 10 it signed non-binding memorandums with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR to raise over $500 billion of outside money for its customers.

Read plainly: a company that sells chips is now spending its own balance sheet on power stations, land and financing. That is what a seller does when the thing stopping its product is no longer only the product.

The buyers keep hiring chip designers

Anthropic hired the founder of Google'sⓘ custom chip programme to lead its own semiconductor work, reported on August 21. It still buys chips from NVIDIA, Google and Amazon. In the same reporting it says it is short of data-centre capacity, and it has placed about $250 million of first orders with the startup Fractile plus capacity agreements with Riot Platforms and Volta Infra Holdings.

Microsoft is doing the same at larger scale. On August 11 it was reported to be negotiating factory capacity for upward of 300,000 of its own Maia 300 chips for delivery in 2027, with a longer-term goal above one million units. Every one of those is a chip it does not buy from a merchant supplier.

The money behind all of this keeps growing. UBS said on August 22 that the largest cloud buyers will spend about $4.1 trillion on AI infrastructure from 2026 to 2028, roughly triple the $1.3 trillion of the prior six years. Demand is not the problem, and it has not been for a long time.

What to watch next

The one thing that would genuinelyⓘ lift the ceiling is more good memory coming off the lines. Samsung's yield on its newest stacked memory reached close to 80% as of August 10, up from below 60% when volume production began in February, and SK Hynix's yield has separately touched 80%. Watch whether that turns into delivered volume rather than a lab number. Watch, too, which memory specification NVIDIA settles on for Rubin Ultra: four options have been under review since the third quarter, and that single choice decides how much memory each shipped machine consumes.

For a monthly read on whether more memory is actually leaving the country that makes most of it, watch Korea. Its semiconductor exports nearly tripled from a year earlier to $26 billion in the first 20 days of August, 47.2% of the country's $55.2 billion of total exports. If the next reading flattens, the memory line is not getting any longer.

Nothing on the calendar forces a resolution. NVIDIA's own newsroom carried no earnings date and no pricing announcement in the two weeks to August 20. So the next real signal is likely to be a price: either the rental rates on Lambda's list starting to slide, or a memory yield number that finally shows up as shipped parts.
