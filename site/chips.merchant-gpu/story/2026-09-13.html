More machines shipped, and still not enough

Oracle handed over three times as many GPUs as it did last quarter, yet buyers are still queueing. The first hint of relief comes from an odd place: models that need less memory.

1

Output really did go up

For months the honest answer toⓘ "why isn't supply catching up" was that not enough finished machines were leaving the factories. This week that part changed. Oracle said it delivered more than 300,000 GPUs to AI customers in its latest quarter, nearly triple the previous quarter, and switched on 850 megawatts of extra data-centre capacity. Its cloud infrastructure revenue rose 121 percent to $7.4 billion.

The picture upstream matches. Grace Blackwell shipments are reported up 27 percent from the month before, with rack systems now leaving the line at hundreds of units a week. TSMC's August sales came in at NT$514.81 billion, up 53.3 percent on a year earlier and 10.1 percent on July. The chain is moving faster than it was.

2

The queue got longer anyway

Even after tripling deliveries, Oracle saidⓘ customer demand for AI training and inference keeps running past the capacity it has. Microsoft is short enough that it has turned cloud and AI customers away, limited subscriptions and absorbed service failures, including an eight-hour GitHub outage in August tied to server shortages. Its answer is to grow total compute from roughly 12 gigawatts today to more than 38 gigawatts by 2032.

Nvidia's chief executive put the same arithmetic plainly: the company is confident of growing revenue 70 percent year over year while unconstrained customer demand grows more than 100 percent a year. He also listed what is holding things back, and the list is long — chips, memory, packaging, power, data centres, and further down the line, land, electricity and buildings. No single fix clears all of it.

3

Relief from an unexpected direction

The bottleneck everyone has been watchingⓘ is memory. The first crack in it came not from a new factory but from a model. DeepSeek said its AI models now need less high-bandwidth memory, and the market took it seriously: SK Hynix shares fell 2.2 percent and Samsung 3.5 percent. If newer models use less memory for each answer they produce, the link between growing usage and growing hardware orders gets weaker.

A second, quieter sign came from Kioxia, whose flash memory prices rose 70 percent in the June quarter after a prior quarter above 100 percent. Its chief executive said another jump of that size looks unlikely, because even the largest buyers have limited budgets. Prices that cannot keep rising are the ordinary way a shortage begins to end.

4

What to watch next

Watch whether the memory relief isⓘ real or local. In China it is going the other way: chipmakers including Huawei and Cambricon have raised prices because of the memory shortage, with Huawei's Ascend 950DT card now above 250,000 yuan, a jump of 20 to 50 percent from quotes given two months earlier. Two different worlds, one shortage.

Watch also who gets to sell into the racks. d-Matrix will fold its own inference chips into Nvidia rack systems, with a tape-out due before the end of 2026 and availability in the last quarter of 2027 — a rival joining the leader's platform rather than fighting it. And watch Washington: the Justice Department is asking whether Nvidia structured its $17 billion licensing deal with Groq to sidestep antitrust review.
