A quiet day, and the new numbers are all rental prices

Nothing arrived to change the memory shortage. What did arrive is a price list: H200 capacity now rents at $3.99 and $4.50 per chip per hour at two providers, while a third still has none to sell.

1

The only fresh numbers today are prices

Little changed today in the supplyⓘ story. The new information is a set of prices, captured today. Jarvislabs lists NVIDIA's H200 chip to rent at $3.99 per chip per hour, with an interruptible version at $1.99. Nebius lists the same hardware at $4.50 per chip per hour on demand and $2.45 for capacity that can be taken back.

Lambda still lists no H200, no GB200 and nothing from AMD. Its newest publicly rentable part is the B200, published at $6.69 per chip per hour. The same generation of hardware sits on one provider's shelf and is missing from another's, which is what a market looks like when capacity is spread unevenly rather than plentiful.

Buying outright is the other half of the picture. A single H200 module is quoted on the street at $30,000 to $40,000, and an eight-GPU server at $350,000 to $420,000. Those are asking prices, not deals done, but they are the numbers a buyer is quoted this month.

Why supply still has not caught up

Nothing arrived today to change theⓘ reason supply lags. Micron said on August 10 that it can supply no more than about half of what customers ask for, called ordinary server memory the number one constraint its largest customers face, and said it has no line of sight to when supply meets demand. That remains the plainest statement anyone in the chain has given.

NVIDIA's own memory suppliers can meet only 60% to 70% of the volume requested for the coming Rubin Ultra generation, reported on August 7. NVIDIA's answer has been to design around the shortage rather than wait it out: the part is being tested at 192GB of memory against a planned 1TB.

The effect shows up in what actually gets built. Large US cloud providers grew server buying 11.6% quarter over quarter in the second quarter, but are forecast to add only 1.7% in the third. Tight supply of memory, processors and the boards that carry them is named as the main brake, so the slowdown reads as a limit on parts rather than a fall in appetite.

What to watch next

The demand side keeps adding names.ⓘ NVIDIA said yesterday that SpaceXAI has committed to its Vera processors and will expand its Grok computing on the Vera Rubin platform as it scales toward gigawatts of capacity. Watch whether that commitment turns into delivered racks or simply joins the queue behind everyone else.

The rental rates are the fastest thermometer anyone has. If H200 hours at $3.99 and $4.50 drift down over the coming weeks, that is the first honest sign capacity has loosened. If they hold or climb, the shortage is intact whatever else is announced.

The one change that would genuinely lift the ceiling is more good memory coming off the lines. Samsung's yield on its newest stacked memory reached close to 80%, up from below 60% when volume production began, roughly four months ahead of the expected timeline. Watch whether that turns into parts on ships rather than a laboratory result.
