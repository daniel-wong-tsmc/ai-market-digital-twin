Behind the verdict: Big buyers stop waiting and go shopping for other chips

2026-08-22

Big buyers stop waiting and go shopping for other chips

Memory is still the thing that caps how many chips get built, so Google, AMD and Cerebras all spent this week buying or building alternatives instead.

1

The cap is still memory, and now the small parts too

Nothing has changed about why supplyⓘ is not catching up. NVIDIA's memory suppliers can meet only 60% to 70% of the stacked-memory volume asked of them for the Rubin Ultra generation. Micron said at its August 10 forum that it can supply no more than about half of customer demand, calls this memory the number one constraint its largest customers face, and has no line of sight to when supply meets demand. TrendForce reported on August 4 that DRAM supply stays tight through 2027, which limits how much factory capacity can be turned over to stacked memory at all.

The squeeze keeps spreading down the parts list. GlobalWafers said on August 6 that its 300mm, 200mm and 150mm lines are effectively at full use, with shortages already showing on some advanced 12-inch products. New this week: Digitimes reported on August 17 that lead times for the high-specification capacitors used in AI servers are stretching toward 40 weeks. These are cheap components, and a server does not ship without them.

The packaging step that bonds these parts together is the one place that may have eased, and even there the sources disagree. Reporting in early July put the shortfall narrowing from roughly 20% to about 10% by the end of 2026. An August 5 report still put the gap near 20% for the year, even with monthly capacity climbing toward 130,000 to 140,000 wafers.

2

Customers quietly line up chips that are not GPUs

The clearest move of the weekⓘ came from Google. On August 18 it widened a custom-chip agreement with Marvell, backed by a warrant whose vesting is tied to revenue steps implying a ceiling of roughly $120 billion. Google is adding Marvell inference parts and near-memory compute alongside its own in-house chip programme. One read of the deal is that Marvell is taking work from Broadcom rather than the industry spending more, so the news is about who supplies the volume, not whether the volume exists.

AMD made the same bet with its wallet. On August 6 it agreed to buy Taalas, a Toronto firm that builds silicon purpose-made for running finished models, and said it will fold that technology into its own GPUs, racks, server chips and software. AMD calls this kind of work one of the fastest-growing parts of the market. Chips that hardwire a model into the silicon could take the cheap, high-volume end of that work away from general-purpose GPUs.

And a rival rack is now shipping. Cerebras launched its CS-4 wafer-scale system on August 19, claiming more than 4,400 words per second for a single user on an open model, up to 30 times the per-user speed of GPU-based systems and up to ten times the output per unit of electricity of its own last generation, with first shipments this quarter. It names OpenAI, G42 and AWS among its customers. None of this replaces the GPU order book today, but every one of these buyers is building a second door.

3

What to watch next

Rental prices are the fastest readⓘ on whether any of this is loosening, and today they look calm. RunPod listed an H100 at $3.29 per chip per hour on its secure cloud, and CoreWeave listed an eight-chip H100 machine at $49.24 an hour, both captured today with no earlier figure to compare against. Watch whether those numbers start to slide, because cheaper rentals would be the first sign that supply has caught up.

The real relief would come from more good memory coming off the lines. Samsung's yield on its newest stacked memory has reached close to 80%, up from below 60% when volume production started in February 2026 and about four months ahead of the expected timeline, and SK Hynix's yield has separately touched 80%. Watch whether that holds as volume climbs. Watch, too, which memory specification NVIDIA settles on for Rubin Ultra: TrendForce says four options have been under review since the third quarter, and that choice decides how much memory each shipped system eats.

Two smaller signals stay in view. NVIDIA's chief executive has held early talks with the Korean chip designer Rebellions about a partnership, investment or purchase, which would be another step into building rather than buying. And on August 20 NVIDIA denied a report that it would ship a China-specific part by year end, saying no such chip is on its roadmap, so the China channel should not be counted on for relief.
