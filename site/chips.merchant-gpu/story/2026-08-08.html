Behind the verdict: Memory makers can fill only two-thirds of the order

2026-08-08

Memory makers can fill only two-thirds of the order

NVIDIA's memory suppliers can cover 60% to 70% of what it asked for its next generation, so it is cutting memory per chip instead. Today's price sweep shows what that scarcity costs: an H200 still fetches $30,000 to $40,000, a single B300 about $53,000.

1

The suppliers can meet two-thirds of what was asked

Seoul Economic Daily reported on 7ⓘ August that NVIDIA's memory suppliers can meet only 60% to 70% of the stacked-memory volume it requested for the Rubin Ultra generation. That one number explains most of the gap between what buyers want and what actually arrives.

NVIDIA's answer is to put less memory on each chip. The same report says it is testing Rubin Ultra at 192 gigabytes and 256 gigabytes, down from a planned 1 terabyte, with some samples reverting to earlier-generation parts. A news flash on 3 August put the mainline part at 192 gigabytes in an eight-high stack, down from 288 gigabytes in a twelve-high stack, and named memory supply and data-centre power limits as the reasons.

TrendForce said on 4 August that memory supply stays short through 2027, which caps how much plant capacity the memory makers can turn over to stacked memory, and that NVIDIA has been weighing four different memory specifications for Rubin Ultra since this quarter. Less memory per chip means more finished chips out of the same pool. It does not mean more memory.

2

What the shortage costs, in dollars

Today's price sweep puts an NVIDIAⓘ H200 at $30,000 to $40,000 per unit across resellers, against a list price of $40,000 to $55,000. A single B300 is quoted at roughly $53,000.

Renting tells the same story. Across 13 provider setups the B300 comes in at a median of $8.13 per GPU-hour, starting at $7.39, and RunPod's own published price of 27 July was that same $7.39. Lambda lists the older B200 at $6.69 per GPU-hour on an eight-card machine.

The market is not settled. Two aggregators quote the GB300 at $8.62 and at $4.31 per GPU-hour for the same part. When one chip carries prices a factor of two apart, buyers are not shopping in a single market. They are taking whatever they can get.

Buyers start booking the raw material years early

Tom's Hardware reported on 7 Augustⓘ that GlobalWafers, which makes the blank silicon discs every chip and every memory stack starts on, has effectively reached maximum use of its 300mm, 200mm and 150mm lines, with shortages showing up on some advanced 300mm products.

Micron committed $500 million to GlobalWafers' Texas plant under a ten-year supply agreement. That sits inside a raised $250 billion US spending plan aimed at making 40% of its memory chips in the United States by 2035.

Buying habits are changing too. Wafer buyers are rebuilding safety stock and stretching contracts from about three years to five-to-eight years, and spot prices for 300mm wafers have started to rise. Locking up plain silicon that far ahead is what customers do when they do not expect the shortage to end soon.

4

What to watch from here

First, which memory setup NVIDIA settlesⓘ on for Rubin Ultra. TrendForce says four specifications have been under review since this quarter, and that choice decides how many finished chips one pool of memory can fill.

Second, whether the bare-silicon squeeze widens. Spot 300mm wafer prices have begun rising and buyers are already stretching contracts out to five-to-eight years, so any further move there lands underneath everything else.

Third, whether the second supplier actually delivers. AMD guided the current quarter to about $13.0 billion, plus or minus $300 million, roughly 41% above last year, and that is the plainest test of whether a rival is shipping rather than announcing. Until one of those three moves, a $53,000 price on a single B300 says the queue has not cleared.
