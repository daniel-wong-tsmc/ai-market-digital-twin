Behind the verdict: The memory makers start to move

2026-09-07

The memory makers start to move

SK Hynix is pushing its newest memory toward a third of its own output, while OpenAI's latest model shows how far ahead the demand for machines still runs.

1

Demand gets a bigger number

OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra. NVIDIA's chiefⓘ executive said the model was trained on more than 100,000 Grace Blackwell rack systems, and that a further wave of chips is due to come online next. That is one customer, one model, and a number most buyers cannot get near.

The buying keeps pace with the boasting. Anthropic has signed about $517 billion of computing agreements in the past eleven months, far above the roughly $180 billion of rented-server spending it had guided investors toward through 2029, and its committed capacity sits with a few providers, with Google and Amazon's cloud arm together holding 11 gigawatts of it.

None of that changes the ceiling. At its late-August results NVIDIA said it holds supply for only about 70% of demand and that the whole chain is running flat out, and it guided next year to about 70% revenue growth when customer forecasts implied roughly 140%, because memory could not cover the rest.

2

The part that has been holding everything up

Today brought the first real supplyⓘ news in a while, and it came from the memory side. SK Hynix is speeding up its newest generation of memory, aiming for it to be about 34% of the company's own memory production by the last quarter of 2026, as it prepares for the next generation of the stacked memory that goes on these chips.

It is not alone. Micron plans to add up to 60,000 stacked-memory wafers a month by the end of 2026, taking it to roughly 100,000 a month from 40,000 to 50,000 last year. The trade remains in very few hands: on the figures in that report, SK Hynix held 50% of the stacked-memory market in the second quarter of 2026, Samsung 32% and Micron 18%.

Two cautions. These are production plans, not shipped parts, and the same report notes that the wider memory league table is contested, with Samsung ahead of SK Hynix on memory revenue share as of mid-2026. And the scale of what is needed is set by the buyer: NVIDIA said in late August that its long-term supply promises had risen to $279 billion, mainly to buy stacked memory and server memory for the Vera Rubin build.

At the counter, and out the back door

The shop window says nothing hasⓘ loosened yet. Newegg listed a PNY NVIDIA H100 80GB card at $34,879, with configuration options from $29,500, and the only true H200 product on its page was out of stock with no price at all. The same check a day earlier found most H100-class listings out of stock and the H200 rack card shown only as coming soon.

Machines that scarce are worth moving quietly. A New York Times investigation reports that $5.6 billion of advanced AI technology, including more than $3 billion of NVIDIA Blackwell-based servers, was allegedly routed to China between April 2024 and February 2026 through Inspur's American subsidiary Aivres. The claim is an allegation, not a finding of guilt.

It matters to the supply question because the official channel is shut. NVIDIA assumes no data-centre computing revenue from China at all in its outlook for the current quarter, so anything reaching that market is coming from somewhere other than a sale.

What to watch next

Watch whether today's memory plans turnⓘ into parts. SK Hynix's 34% target lands in the last quarter of this year and Micron's extra wafers by the end of it, so the next few months will show whether the promises are real. Set against that, SK Hynix's own chief has said the shortage could run to 2030.

Watch NVIDIA's next set of results. It has guided to $108 billion of revenue for the coming quarter, with its newest platform expected to be about 20% of data-centre sales, roughly $20 billion. If that number lands while shops stay empty, the shortfall is still in the parts, not the orders.

And watch what Washington does with the smuggling reports. An allegation of $5.6 billion of hardware reaching China through an American subsidiary invites tighter rules, and tighter rules on where machines may go do nothing to make more of them.
