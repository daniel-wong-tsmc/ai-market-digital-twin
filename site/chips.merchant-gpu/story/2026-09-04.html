The shortage is no longer just about memory

Chip plants, buildings and power are all short at once, and buyers keep signing anyway.

1

More than one thing is short

The reason supply is not catchingⓘ up changed shape this week. At a Taipei industry event on 2 September, TSMC said it still cannot meet chip demand even while it builds about 20 plants at the same time, naming construction labour shortages and slow equipment deliveries, and expecting the squeeze to last into 2027.

The scale of the catch-up effort is in its own order book: TSMC's quarterly demand for factory tools now runs at roughly 1.9 times the level it projected back in December 2025. Buying more machines has not closed the gap.

Broadcom's management made the same point in plainer words on 2 September. Land, power and finished building shells now decide when new capacity switches on, alongside packaging material, stacked memory and leading-edge wafers. Fixing one of those does not help while another is missing.

Buyers keep signing anyway

Broadcom reported $16.7 billion of chipⓘ revenue for artificial-intelligence work in its third quarter, up 221% on a year earlier and 54% on the quarter before, and told investors it has line of sight to roughly $230 billion by fiscal 2028.

The order books behind the chips are also filling. Hewlett Packard Enterprise booked record artificial-intelligence orders of $3.1 billion in its fiscal third quarter, $2.4 billion of it systems and about $700 million networking gear, on revenue of $12.2 billion, up 34%. It raised its full-year sales growth range to 34% to 37%.

Smaller operators are signing too. Nscale told prospective investors its contracted revenue has roughly doubled to about $103 billion, and on 3 September it signed the robot maker Figure to an initial $3.5 billion of compute, with stated intent to pass $6 billion and to run up to 100,000 chips in Barstow, Texas from the second half of 2027.

Whoever holds the memory holds the schedule

Almost none of the memory isⓘ loose. Samsung has tied up about 70% of its capacity in long-term deals with buyers including Microsoft, NVIDIA and Google that run to 2031, and the small uncontracted channel is dear: spot stacked memory trades at roughly four to five times the long-term contract price. SK Hynix has committed $38.3 billion of investment through 2031, including a $4 billion packaging plant in Indiana aimed at 2029 production, and its chief says the shortage could last through 2030.

Governments are now writing cheques against the same scarce parts. South Korea announced a $919 billion plan to build 8.4 gigawatts of data-centre computing by 2029, rising to 18.4 gigawatts by 2035, with SK Group taking 2 gigawatts of NVIDIA Rubin systems and Naver a 200-megawatt order, because Korea has no home-grown supplier of a top-end accelerator. The same report says NVIDIA may have secured a long-term memory agreement with SK Hynix plus better pricing for 2027, which would move terms in the chip vendor's favour rather than add supply.

The scarcity is visible at the retail counter. Newegg today shows a single H100 card listed at $34,879, with other sellers from $29,500, while every other comparable listing on the page is out of stock or marked coming soon.

4

What to watch next

Watch the new claims on theⓘ same memory. NVIDIA has revived its Rubin CPX design for production in the first quarter of 2027, and the revived part is reported to carry 168 gigabytes of stacked memory in place of the 128 gigabytes of ordinary graphics memory in the earlier design. Each new design that wants stacked memory pushes another one down.

Watch for supply that does not depend on the same three sellers. China's CXMT has started small-batch production of an earlier-generation stacked memory, a generation behind the leaders, with broader availability aimed at 2027, and Alibaba's chip arm and Cambricon are already testing it. Intel is taking the other route: its Crescent Island inference part carries up to 480 gigabytes of ordinary low-power memory instead.

And watch who is being locked in while parts are scarce. AMD's MI355X systems went live in Saudi Arabia on 31 August, with up to 250 megawatts planned from 2027. NVIDIA agreed on 3 September to buy Hugging Face, the main open-model distribution site, and has put $3.5 billion into MediaTek convertible bonds to pull a third designer onto its own connection fabric. If supply stays short into 2027, the contest shifts from who can ship to who has already tied up the customer.
