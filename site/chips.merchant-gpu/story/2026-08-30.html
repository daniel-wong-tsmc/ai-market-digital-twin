The memory shortage picks up a labour problem

Demand keeps setting records, but the memory shortage that caps how many chips ship now has union trouble on top of it, and the new capacity being built is years away.

1

Demand is still far ahead of what can be built

NVIDIA sold $89.0 billion of data-centreⓘ chips in its July quarter, up 117% from a year earlier, and told investors to expect $108.0 billion this quarter.

On the same August 26 call, the company said it has supply for only about 70% of the demand it sees, and that the whole supply chain is running flat out. Amazon Web Services said the same day it will add 2 million more NVIDIA chips across its data centres in 2027 and 2028, on top of an earlier one-million commitment that demand had already outrun.

The people who make the memory chips are now a risk

The part that caps how manyⓘ AI chips can be built is memory, and this week the trouble moved from the factories to the people inside them. SK hynix's union rejected its wage agreement in a vote held on August 25 and 26, with 50.08% against and 49.92% in favour, a margin of 25 votes out of 15,045 ballots cast on 93.81% turnout.

At Micron, about 80% of unionised workers back labour action over bonus terms. Taiwan holds roughly 60% of Micron's worldwide production capacity, mandatory mediation is expected to begin in mid-September 2026, and a formal strike vote becomes possible that month if the talks fail. By contrast, Samsung's union ratified its own deal back in May 2026 with 73.7% in favour.

3

The fixes are real, and they are years away

SK hynix broke ground on Augustⓘ 28 on its first American plant for packaging high-bandwidth memory, in West Lafayette, Indiana, backed by more than $4 billion. The cleanroom is expected to open by October 2028, and mass production of next-generation memory there is scheduled for the second half of 2029. Even then, the wafers will still be made in South Korea and shipped to Indiana for packaging and testing.

Kioxia and Sandisk separately said they plan to invest more than $31 billion in Japan through 2032 to expand flash memory capacity for AI demand, building on their existing Yokkaichi joint venture. That spending is contingent on government support, so it is a plan rather than a commitment.

4

What to watch next

The first date to mark isⓘ mid-September 2026, when mediation between Micron and its Taiwan union is expected to start, with a strike vote possible that month if it goes badly. Taiwan holds roughly 60% of Micron's production capacity, so the outcome matters well beyond one company.

In Washington, two things are being written but neither is law yet. The Commerce Department is reported to be drafting a rule that would stop Chinese AI firms renting NVIDIA chip time through data centres in Southeast Asia; export-control lawyers quoted in that account say the administration may lack the legal authority to enforce it, and no published rule, docket or start date has been found. Separately, the administration is reported to be preparing tariffs on imported chips that would extend to finished data-centre servers, with duty-free import allowances sized to how much a company invests in US chip plants. No rate and no start date have been given, and the account says the plan could still change substantially.

One more thread to follow is who pays. Reporting says NVIDIA has paused certain deals under a compute-partnership financing programme covering $36 billion of commitments on six-year agreements; NVIDIA denies the programme is halted and says the underlying model stays in place. If vendor credit support shrinks, some of the order book behind these shipments thins with it.
