The memory shortage finally shows up on the invoice

Nothing about the supply of stacked memory got better. What changed is the price: the seller is reported to be raising machine prices more than 15%, and the memory maker says customers want half again more than it can promise.

1

Prices, not deliveries, are what moved

NVIDIA is reported to be raisingⓘ the price of its AI server systems by more than 15% in many cases, for machines shipping from early next year. That was reported on August 22. The reason given is cost, not greed: contract prices for ordinary computer memory have risen roughly 50% so far this year, and that memory now sits as a large line in the bill for every AI server built.

The size of these machines makes the increase real money. A rack of the current generation is cited at $2.8 million to $3.4 million per unit. Enquiries ahead of shipment for the next generation are running at $5 million to $7 million per unit. A rise of more than 15% on top of that is a serious number for any buyer planning a campus.

Read plainly: when a seller can raise prices this far into a buildout and expect buyers to pay, it is telling you supply has not caught up. Nobody discounts a product they cannot make enough of.

2

The memory maker puts a number on the gap

Micron's chief executive said, in reportingⓘ dated August 23, that customers are asking for about 50% more memory than the company can commit to supply. That is the clearest measure yet of the gap, and it comes from the seller rather than an outside estimate. Micron also projects about $22 billion of cash deposits and related commitments under signed agreements where the buyer pays whether or not it takes delivery.

That fits what NVIDIA's own suppliers have said. Reported on August 7, they can meet only 60% to 70% of the stacked-memory volume asked of them for the Rubin Ultra generation. Buyers paying deposits years ahead, and suppliers filling two-thirds of the order, describe the same shortage from opposite ends.

There is some engineering progress. At a chip conference on August 23, Samsung showed a design that stacks four memory units directly on top of a 1,200-watt accelerator, saving roughly 100 watts while carrying more data. That is a better way to use memory, not more memory. It does not change what can be shipped this year.

3

Nine people go on trial over where the chips went

Today Taiwan's Keelung prosecutors indicted nineⓘ people, including employees of Nvidia and Super Micro, over allegedly exporting AI servers carrying controlled Nvidia chips to China and other destinations. This is the first time this case has produced named charges rather than raids.

Super Micro's own board looked into the matter and reported on August 21. It found no knowledge among current senior managers and no direct sales to restricted parties, but the company fired staff and widened its export-compliance work anyway. The underlying US indictment covers roughly $2.5 billion of servers.

For a buyer, the point is simple. Some share of the machines that get built is spoken for by rules about who may own them, and the people who move them are now facing criminal charges. That keeps a slice of the world's demand permanently off the table for the legal supply.

4

What to watch next

Watch whether the reported price riseⓘ of more than 15% actually sticks when buyers get their quotes for early next year. If buyers pay it without argument, the shortage is worse than the headline numbers suggest. If they push back, that is the first sign the balance is turning.

Rental prices are the fastest check, and today they are unmoved. Lambda's list, captured today, shows its B200 chip at $6.69 per chip per hour, and it still offers no H200, no GB200 and nothing from AMD. CoreWeave, also captured today, publishes its current top-end rack at $42.00 an hour. When rented capacity starts to look plentiful, these are the first numbers that will fall.

The one thing that would genuinely lift the ceiling is more good memory coming off the lines. Korean production quality reached close to 80% as of August 10, up from below 60% when volume production began in February. Watch whether that turns into parts on ships rather than a laboratory result.
