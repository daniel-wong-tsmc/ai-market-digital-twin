The biggest seller can fill only seven orders in ten

NVIDIA's own numbers show record sales and a shrinking profit margin at the same time, because memory costs more than it expected. Fresh money for memory factories arrived today, but it is the wrong kind and it runs to 2032.

1

Record sales, and a margin bent by memory prices

NVIDIA reported $89.0 billion of dataⓘ center revenue for its second quarter on August 26, up 117 percent from a year earlier, and told investors to expect about $108.0 billion in the current quarter. On any ordinary reading that is a company selling everything it can make.

The telling detail sits next to it. NVIDIA guided its gross margin down to 74.0 percent. On the same call management said memory pricing has been extreme, worse than the company itself expected, and is set to rise again next year. So the market leader is paying more for the parts it cannot do without, and it is showing up in the profit on each machine.

Management also said plainly that it has supply for about 70 percent of demand and that the whole chain is running flat out. That single sentence is the answer to why supply is not catching up: it is not caution, and it is not lack of orders. The company also assumes no data center revenue from China at all in this outlook.

New money for memory, but not the memory that binds

Today brought the first sizeable supply-sideⓘ answer in a while. Kioxia and Sandisk said they will invest more than $31 billion in Japan through 2032, subject to government support, to expand flash memory output for AI demand at their shared Yokkaichi plant.

Two things temper that. The spend runs to 2032, so it does nothing for a machine anyone wants to build this year or next. And flash is not the stacked memory that sits beside the processor and decides how many accelerators actually ship.

On that stacked memory, nothing has loosened. Micron said on August 10 that it can supply no more than about half of what customers ask for, called memory the number one constraint its largest customers face, and said it has no line of sight to when supply meets demand.

Orders keep arriving in blocks nobody can quietly absorb

Amazon Web Services said on Augustⓘ 26 it will deploy 2 million more NVIDIA accelerators across its sites in 2027 and 2028. That is one buyer, one announcement, and a number that dwarfs anything the memory chain is adding this decade.

The day before, Anthropic was reported to have committed roughly $45 billion over six years to Nscale for capacity built on NVIDIA systems. Model builders are still able to raise the money to buy compute well ahead of the revenue it will earn.

Both point at the same uncomfortable shape. Near-term demand sits with a small number of very large buyers, with a single cloud committing millions of units. That keeps the order book full, and it also means one of them changing its mind moves a very large block at once.

4

What to watch next

A new cost may be aboutⓘ to land on top of the memory bill. The Trump administration is reported today to be weighing sweeping tariffs on semiconductors and on tech products including data center servers. Nothing is confirmed, and no rate has been named, so treat it as a risk to watch rather than a fact to price.

Rental rates remain the quickest thermometer. Runpod listed its newest Blackwell part, the B300, at $7.89 per chip per hour today. If that number drifts down over the coming weeks, capacity has genuinely loosened; if it holds or climbs, the shortage is intact whatever gets announced.

The one figure that would settle the argument is NVIDIA's own margin. It has guided to 74.0 percent while saying memory prices will rise again next year. Watch whether that guide holds. If it slips further, the memory bill is winning; if it holds, the seller is passing the cost on.
