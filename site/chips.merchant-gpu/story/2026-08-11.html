The memory maker says it can fill half the orders, and has no idea when that changes

Micron told an investor forum yesterday that memory, not chip-making, is the number one thing holding customers back — and that it cannot yet see the point where supply catches demand. Money, meanwhile, keeps arriving.

1

Half the orders, and no date for when that ends

Micron said at an investor forumⓘ on 10 August that it can supply no more than about half of what its customers ask for. It called memory the number one constraint the biggest cloud buyers face today, and said it has no line of sight to when supply will meet demand.

There is a reason the shortage feeds on itself. Making the stacked memory that goes on an AI accelerator eats about three times the factory space of ordinary memory for the same output, and the next version pushes that to four times. Every stack built for an accelerator is ordinary memory not built.

Nothing in the rest of the week contradicts that. Suppliers can still cover only 60 to 70 percent of the volume Nvidia asked for on its next chip generation, and a market researcher said on 4 August that memory will stay short through 2027, which limits how much factory space can go to the stacked kind at all.

Money is not what is missing

On the same day, Nvidia saidⓘ it had signed preliminary agreements with six large financial firms — Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR — to set up platforms meant to bring in more than $500 billion of outside capital for its customers to buy compute with.

Intel also announced a share sale of $15 billion, up to $17.25 billion if the banks take their option, with the proceeds going to general company purposes including new plant. It named the scale of AI compute investment as the reason.

So the funding side is wide open while the physical side is not. That gap is the whole story of this market right now: buyers can pay, lenders will lend, and the parts still are not there.

The biggest customers are quietly building their own way out

Reporting today says Microsoft is negotiatingⓘ factory space for upward of 300,000 of its own Maia 300 chips for delivery in 2027, with a longer-term goal of more than a million. Every one of those it gets is a part it does not have to buy from a merchant vendor.

MediaTek is chasing the same work from the other side. On 3 August it said it expects more than $2 billion of custom AI data-centre chip revenue this year, with its first such chip going into production in the final quarter of 2026 for large US cloud customers.

This does not free up any memory. It does change who is standing in the queue for the scarce parts, and it slowly loosens one vendor's grip on the buyers.

What to watch next

The cleanest early signal is whatⓘ a chip costs to rent or to buy second-hand. Today's published rates show one provider asking $6.69 to $6.99 an hour for a top Blackwell part and $3.99 to $4.29 for the previous generation, while a rival lists the same classes lower, at $5.89 and $2.99. Older hardware still changes hands at $15,000 to $28,000 used and $25,000 to $40,000 new. If the shortage were easing, those numbers would start drifting down together.

Watch two supply numbers over the next few weeks. Whether Micron's roughly half-of-demand fill rate improves at all, and whether the packaging step gets faster: on 5 August a researcher reported the main chipmaker is handing part of that work to outside partners, with Nvidia holding 800,000 to 850,000 wafers of this year's capacity and the shortfall still around 20 percent.

The honest read is that today changed the funding picture, not the physical one. Until a memory supplier can name a date, more money mostly means more people waiting in the same line.
