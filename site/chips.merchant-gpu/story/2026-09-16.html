2026-09-16

Buyers start designing around the memory shortage

Fresh orders landed again this week, but the parts that turn orders into working machines are still the slow step, and the fixes on offer are dated 2027.

1

The orders land faster than the buildings

Anthropic signed a six-year, $13.7 billionⓘ compute agreement with the developer RUM Group, to be served from a new data center in Georgia. The site is the catch: RUM Group disclosed financing trouble in August, and there is now doubt it will hit its late-2026 completion target, which puts delivery of that contract at risk.

The pattern is familiar. Oracle handed over more than 300,000 chips to its own customers last quarter, close to triple the quarter before, and still says demand for training and serving work runs past what it has available. Money and orders are not the shortage. Finished capacity is.

Two ways the memory pinch could ease

Micron showed what it calls theⓘ first 512-gigabyte server memory module, enough to put up to 12 terabytes of memory in a single two-socket machine, and it says the part draws more than 60 percent less operating power than the four smaller modules it replaces. The date is the problem: volume production is not expected until the second half of 2027.

The other route is needing less memory in the first place. DeepSeek said its newer models require less high-bandwidth memory, and shares of the two Korean memory makers fell 2.2 percent and 3.5 percent on the news. If serving an answer takes cheaper memory, the link between rising usage and rising chip purchases gets looser. Neither route helps the machines being built this quarter.

3

The biggest buyers are building their own

Meta will put its third-generation in-houseⓘ chip into data centers in the first half of 2027 and a fourth generation late that year, stated plainly as a way to depend less on Nvidia hardware, and it has committed to more than 1 gigawatt of capacity over the next twelve months. Broadcom, which co-designs parts like these, now projects its own artificial-intelligence chip sales rising from about $58 billion this year to $115 billion in 2027 and $230 billion in 2028. Samsung also co-led a 200-million-euro first round for Euclyd, a Dutch startup building an inference chip that is not a graphics part.

None of this makes the queue shorter. Broadcom's own management says land, power and building shells set the timing of new capacity, alongside packaging substrate, memory and leading-edge wafers. Custom chips change who is waiting in line, not how long the line is.

4

Three things worth watching next

First, dates on the ground. Whetherⓘ RUM Group's Georgia site meets its late-2026 target is the cleanest near-term test of whether signed money turns into serving capacity on schedule.

Second, whether the memory relief is real. Micron's dense module is a 2027 event, and DeepSeek's claim of lighter memory needs is so far a claim that moved share prices rather than a change in what buyers order.

Third, planning. Nvidia and Palantir are putting an artificial-intelligence planning system into Nvidia's own supply chain first, a chain the company describes as spanning millions of parts and thousands of suppliers. If better planning shows up anywhere as extra finished machines, that is where it should appear first.
